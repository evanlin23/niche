COLD OPEN
[o1]  Every family plays Uno a little differently.
[o2]  Somebody stacks their plus twos. Somebody swaps hands on a seven. Somebody makes you keep drawing until you can play. And somebody always says that's not a real rule.

[o4]  One of those rules makes the average round seventy five percent longer. And one round in ten turns into a marathon of more than a hundred and seventy turns.
THE GAME IN TEN SECONDS
[g1]  Quick refresher. Match the top card by color or number, or play a Wild. Action cards skip people, reverse direction, or make them draw. First one out of cards wins the round.

[x4]  The average isn't even the worst part. With the official rules, one round in ten goes past seventy six turns. With draw until you can play, one in ten goes past a hundred and seventy.

EPISODE 3 — CONNECT 4   (~619 words, about 3.3 min at your pace)
========================================================================

COLD OPEN
[o1]  Connect Four was solved in 1988. A computer worked out that the first player can always win, if they start in the middle and never make a mistake.
[o2]  That's no help at all when you're playing your little brother. You can't memorize a solution with trillions of positions.
[o3]  So I built bots that play like people, from complete beginners to pretty sharp players, and had them play millions of games against each other.
[o4]  Here's the headline. One simple habit beats a beginner ninety six games out of a hundred. And looking just two moves ahead beats a careful player more than eight times in ten.
CAN YOU TRUST IT
[c1]  First, the engine. It plays on the standard seven by six grid, drops discs exactly like the real thing, and checks every row, column and diagonal for four in a row.
[c2]  To make sure it understands the game, I gave it the hardest test there is. Solving the whole thing from an empty board. It found the same answer as the 1988 proof. Start in the middle and you can force a win. Start one column over and the best you can do is a draw. Start anywhere else and you lose.
[c3]  Confession: that one check took longer to run than every other experiment in this video combined.
THE LADDER
[l1]  Now the bots. Each one adds a single habit to the one before it.
[l2]  The Beginner drops discs at random. The Blocker takes a win when it sees one, and blocks yours. That one habit beats the Beginner ninety six percent of the time.
[l3]  If you only remember one thing from this video, it's this. Before every move, check for a four you can finish, and a four they can finish.
HABIT TWO: THE MIDDLE
[m1]  The second habit is the middle.
[m2]  There are more ways to make four through the middle column than through any other. So the next bot plays toward the center whenever it can.
[m3]  Against the Blocker, it wins sixty eight percent of games. Same reflexes, just better aim.
HABIT THREE: DON'T SET THEM UP
[s1]  Habit three is the sneaky one. Never play directly underneath a spot where your opponent could win.
[s2]  If you fill the square below their winning square, you just handed them the next move. The Careful bot never does that, and it beats the center bot sixty one percent of the time.
HABIT FOUR: TWO MOVES AHEAD
[t1]  The fourth habit is the big one. Look two moves ahead. For each move you could make, ask what your opponent would do next.
[t2]  That bot beats the Careful bot eighty four percent of the time.
[t3]  And here's the surprise. Looking four or six moves ahead, with only a rough sense of what a good position looks like, didn't reliably beat looking two. My guess is that past a point, it's not about how far you look. It's about knowing what you're looking for.
GOING FIRST
[f1]  What about going first? With perfect play, it's a guaranteed win. With real people, it's a nudge.
[f2]  Between two players who use the middle, the first player wins fifty three percent of games and the second wins forty three. The rest are draws.
[f3]  And where you start matters, even for normal players. Opening in the middle column wins forty nine percent of games for the first player. Opening on an edge, under thirty nine.
THE RECIPE
[w1]  So here's how to beat your friends at Connect Four.
[w2]  Check for wins and blocks every single move. Play toward the middle. Never fill the square under their winning spot. And before you drop a disc, ask what they'll do next.
[w3]  And if you get to go first, start in the middle. The computer from 1988 would approve.
CLOSE
[e1]  Which game should I simulate next? Tell me in the comments, and tell me the house rule your family swears by.

EPISODE 4 — SHUT THE BOX   (~719 words, about 3.8 min at your pace)
========================================================================

COLD OPEN
[o1]  Shut the Box is the pub game with the wooden tiles, numbered one to nine. Roll two dice, flip down tiles that add up to your roll, and try to flip them all.
[o2]  Everyone who plays it has a system. Take the big tiles first. Take as many tiles as you can. Just go with your gut.
[o3]  So I solved it. Every possible board, every possible roll. Then I had bots play over a billion games to make sure the math holds up.
[o4]  Here's the headline. Played perfectly, you shut the box about one game in fourteen. But there's a popular house rule that makes it fourteen times harder. Even for perfect play.
THE RULES IN TEN SECONDS
[g1]  Quick rules. You roll two dice. You flip down any open tiles that add up to the total. If you can't, your turn is over, and the tiles still standing are your score. Shut them all, and you win outright.
CAN YOU TRUST IT
[c1]  Shut the Box is small enough to solve exactly. Nine tiles means only five hundred and twelve possible boards. For each one, the computer checks every roll and every move, and works out which choice gives you the best chance.
[c2]  Then I replayed hundreds of millions of games with the perfect strategy, rolling real random dice. Perfect play shut the box seven point one four percent of the time on paper. In the simulation, seven point one five.
[c3]  Confession. The first time I checked my random player, the math and the simulation disagreed. My exact calculation had frozen one random choice per board instead of averaging over all of them. Fixed, they now agree to a hundredth of a percent.
THE SYSTEMS
[s1]  So let's test the systems people actually use.
[s2]  The Big Spender always takes the biggest tile it can. The Minimalist flips as few tiles as possible. The Sweeper flips as many as possible, cleaning up the small ones first. And the Coin Flipper just picks any legal move.
[s3]  The Big Spender shuts the box six point nine six percent of the time. Perfect play is seven point one four. That's ninety eight percent as good as a computer that knows every board.
[s4]  The Minimalist is right there too, at seven percent. The Coin Flipper manages two.
[s5]  And the Sweeper? One percent. Almost seven times worse than the Big Spender. Flipping lots of small tiles feels productive. My guess is that you need those small tiles at the end, when only small totals are left.
THE HOUSE RULE
[h1]  Now the house rule. In a lot of homes, you can't flip any combination you like. You can flip one tile that matches the total, or the two tiles that match each die. Sum or split. Nothing else.
[h2]  That rule changes everything. Even perfect play only shuts the box half a percent of the time. About one game in two hundred.
[h3]  And strategy barely matters anymore. The Big Spender, the Sweeper and the Coin Flipper all land within a few hundredths of a percent of each other. With sum or split, Shut the Box is basically a lottery.
TWELVE TILES
[t1]  Some boxes go up to twelve.
[t2]  With any combination allowed, perfect play shuts a twelve tile box about once in two hundred and eighty games. With sum or split, about once in three thousand. You could play every day for years and never see it.
THE ONE DIE RULE
[d1]  One more common rule. Once the seven, eight and nine are down, you roll just one die.
[d2]  For perfect play, that helps a little. Seven point four two percent instead of seven point one four.
[d3]  But for the Big Spender, it hurts. It drops to five and a half. My guess is that the one die rule rewards a slightly different plan, and the simple habit doesn't adjust.
PLAYING FOR POINTS
[p1]  Plenty of tables don't play for the shut. They add up the tiles left standing, and the lowest score wins.
[p2]  Same lesson. Perfect play leaves about eleven points on average. The Big Spender, about eleven and a half. The Sweeper leaves twenty four.
THE RECIPE
[w1]  So here's how to play Shut the Box.
[w2]  Take the biggest tile you can, every time. Don't spend your small tiles early. And if your family plays sum or split, stop worrying about strategy, because you're mostly playing the dice.

COLD OPEN
[o1]  Everybody has a Battleship strategy. Hide in the corners. Line your ships up along the edge. Bunch them all together so they're hard to find.

[g1]  Quick rules. Each player hides five ships on a ten by ten grid. You take turns calling out squares. Hit or miss. Sink the whole fleet first and you win.

[c3]  To check the engine, I used plain math. If you shoot completely at random, you finish when you find the last of those seventeen squares. On paper, that takes ninety five point three nine shots on average. Over ten million games, my engine says ninety five point three nine.

[s1]  Here are the shooters.
[s2]  The Beginner fires at random. The Hunter fires at random too, but once it gets a hit, it checks the squares around it until the ship sinks.
[s3]  The Checkerboard does the same thing, but while it's searching, it only fires at every other square, like the black squares on a chess board. The smallest ship is two squares long, so it can't hide between them.
[s4]  And the Calculator. Before every shot, it counts every way the remaining ships could still fit on the board, and fires where the most of them overlap.

[r2]  The Hunter needs sixty two. That one habit, checking around a hit, beats the Beginner ninety nine games out of a hundred.
[r3]  The Checkerboard needs fifty five, and beats the Hunter sixty five percent of the time.
[r4]  And the Calculator needs forty four. Against the Checkerboard, it wins eighty percent of games.

[h3]  The best thing is to spread them out, so no two ships touch. That wins sixty percent of games against the same opponent.
[h4]  Corners and edges? Against a Hunter, they barely matter at all.
AGAINST THE CALCULATOR
[k1]  But the Calculator is different.
[k2]  It knows ships fit more easily in the middle of the board, so it shoots there first. Against the Calculator, ships on the edges win fifty four percent of games, and ships in the corners win fifty five.
[k3]  So the corner people were half right. Corners help, but only against a really good shooter.
THE HOUSE RULE
[x1]  And remember that rule about naming the ship you hit? A lot of families just say hit.
[x2]  It barely matters. The Calculator needs about half a shot more. Everyone else doesn't use the name anyway.
GOING FIRST
[f1]  Does going first help? A little. Between two equal shooters, the first player wins between fifty one and fifty two percent of games.
[f2]  That's because if you both need the same number of shots, you get there first.
THE RECIPE
[w1]  So here's how to win at Battleship.
[w2]  When you shoot, search in a checkerboard pattern, and when you get a hit, finish that ship before you go back to searching.
[w3]  When you hide, spread your ships out and never let them touch. If your opponent is good, push them toward the edges.
CLOSE
[e1]  Where do you hide your Carrier? Tell me in the comments.
